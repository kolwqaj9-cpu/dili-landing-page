Where every figure in that letter came from

Every number in the letter is computed from one public source: the United States Department of Labor's electronic Form 5500 filings for plan year 2025, and specifically Schedule A, the part of the filing that reports insurance information — the carrier, the contract or policy number, the number of people covered, and the compensation paid to the broker or agent on that contract.

Nothing in the letter comes from a private database, a purchased list, a credit bureau, or anyone inside your company. We did not speak to your broker, your carrier or your employees. We read the same filing that you can download yourself from the Department of Labor's public disclosure site.

The arithmetic, in the order the letter states it:

  1. Total compensation is the sum of the compensation fields across every Schedule A line filed under your plan's EIN for that plan year.
  2. Participants is the participant count reported on the plan's Form 5500 for the same year.
  3. Compensation per participant is the first number divided by the second. It is a ratio, not a bill, and not an accusation.
  4. The comparison group is every plan in the same public dataset that falls in the same participant-size band and the same carrier-contract-count band as your plan. The dataset we worked from contained 14,741 plans that met three inclusion conditions: between 50 and 6,000 participants at the start of the plan year, at least one usable Schedule A line, and reported broker compensation of at least $100,000 summed across those lines. Plans outside those conditions were excluded before any percentile was computed, and no plan was added afterwards.
  5. The percentile is the share of that comparison group whose compensation per participant is strictly lower than yours. It is a rank inside a group of real filings, not a model, not an estimate, and not an interpolation between two points.

Every one of those five steps can be repeated by anyone with the public file and a spreadsheet. That is the point of the exercise. A claim you cannot re-derive is worth nothing, and we would rather be checked than believed.

Why the letter asks you for nothing

Most mail that mentions a number wants something back: a call, a meeting, a signature, a login. That letter does not. It does not ask you to confirm anything, click anything, register for anything or reply at all. If the figures match what you already knew, the correct response is to throw it away — the letter says that too, in its own last paragraph.

We wrote it that way because a letter that asks for nothing is the only kind that can be read without suspicion. What we get out of it is narrower than a sale: we find out whether the arithmetic is worth anyone's attention.

If you want to check the arithmetic on your own plan

Write to tony@dilisights.com and name the plan. We will send back the Schedule A lines we used — carrier, contract number, covered count, compensation — so that you can tie each one to the filing. There is no charge for that and no form to fill in. If a figure is wrong, we want to know first.

One thing this page will not tell you

It will not tell you that your plan is overpaying. We do not know that. A high figure relative to a peer group has innocent explanations — a self-funded medical plan, COBRA administration, a high-claims year that moved the commission into a higher tier — and none of those variables appear in the public filing. The letter tells you where you sit. Whether that is a problem is a question for you and the people who advise you, and it is a question we are not qualified to answer.

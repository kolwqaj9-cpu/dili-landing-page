What we publish under a permanent number.

A website can claim anything about itself. Words on a page are cheap, and we are not exempt from that. So whenever we finish a piece of work worth standing behind, we deposit it in a public archive and take the number that comes with it.

Why we deposit instead of just publishing here

An archived record gets a DOI — a digital object identifier. It is registered with a central agency, it does not change, and it cannot be quietly deleted or edited by whoever wrote the document. A page on our own domain can be rewritten tomorrow; an archived record with a number cannot.

That is the whole point. A claim of being a real, operating company is only worth something if a reader can check it somewhere we do not control. The archive is one of those places, together with the public business register, the phone number and the post address on our company page.

Buyers, payment processors, advertising platforms and search systems keep asking the same question about a supplier, a publisher or a website owner: does a real, reachable organisation stand behind this name, this address and this domain? This report treats that question as a data problem, because almost all of the evidence needed to answer it is already published.

It compiles 28 open sources — company registers, federal procurement and financial datasets, environmental and safety enforcement records, labour statistics, intellectual-property registers and international cross-border systems — each with its live endpoint and refresh cadence. It then sets out a five-signal verification ladder running from statutory registration up to independent third-party records, with weights and stop conditions. Finally it works a complete, reproducible example on a real New Mexico limited liability company, so that every check can be repeated by a reader.

The finding we keep coming back to: verification rarely fails because evidence is missing. It fails because the evidence sits in ten unrelated systems and nobody reconciles it.
